Vibe-coded, unreviewed, and one step from real users

A friend sent me a screenshot of a Next.js app he vibe-coded in about four hours: users trade memes with digital money. There is no payment integration yet, so technically nothing bad can happen. But that is the point.

Proofs of concept have a way of quietly becoming "let us just test it with a few people," which becomes "let us open it up a bit," and nobody ever actually decides to skip the security review. It just never becomes the next step, because the demo already works and that feels good enough. Auth edge cases, race conditions, what happens when someone tries to break it on purpose: the boring stuff that matters the moment real users or real dollars show up. Vibe coding is great for speed. It is not a replacement for someone in the loop who knows what they are looking at.

Why low-level knowledge matters more now, not less

Spending time in the kernel, C, C++, and Rust changes how you think about code. Now I write Python, Java, and JavaScript differently: I think about what happens when a function returns, who owns a piece of memory, and what happens if untrusted input reaches it.

As agents write more of the first draft, fewer people are asking what the code actually does under the hood. Understanding memory, ownership, and what your runtime is doing on your behalf is what lets you spot the class of bug an agent will not flag for you. The cost of skipping this is not abstract: it is cloud bills that creep up for no visible reason, and the breach that starts with one overlooked buffer.

GitHub stars are the new Instagram likes

I saw a two-day-old GitHub repo, just a few README files for Claude, with 50+ stars and 25+ forks. The most stars I have ever gotten was 30 on a Python interpreter I built six years ago that no one uses.

Stars used to be a benchmark for expertise. Now they feel like Instagram likes. The community is growing, but most of it lives at the surface. We are shipping an insane amount of code; the real question is whether it is good code you can still maintain in production four or five years from now.

Touch grass. Build something. Ignore the noise.

"Software engineers are cooked." "Designers are dead." "I built 100 micro-SaaS in a day." "Run 5 agents to 10x your workflow." Same recycled hype, different day.

Here is what nobody profiting from the fear wants to say out loud: the engineers who are cooked always were, taste is harder to fake than ever, and "100 micro-SaaS in a day" is 100 dead repos. The people actually shipping are not posting about it; they are shipping. AI is genuinely changing things, slowly and unevenly, in ways that take more than a hook and a hashtag to explain.

If you can't code without AI, you're not an engineer yet

AI can help you produce thousands of lines a day and ship faster, but that alone does not make someone an engineer. You would not call someone a mechanical engineer just because they can tighten a loose screw.

Engineering is knowing the layers beneath the code: hardware, operating systems, networking, databases, security, and distributed systems. Speed is not engineering. Understanding why systems fail, why performance degrades at scale, and how components interact under real conditions is. This was my most-read post, with 130 reactions.

Most meaningful companies don't start with revolutions

After the AI Summit, a lot of people dismissed small Indian startups as non-innovative. But I spent my time in the startup section and met founders who deeply understand the problems they are solving and are already generating revenue, which is far harder than building demos.

Speaking as someone who has done B2B sales, closing real deals is no small feat. Most meaningful companies start by solving specific, unglamorous problems close to customers, consistently and sustainably. The breakthroughs usually come later.

Feb 2026

Make bots economically unviable instead of asking "are you human?"

Bot abuse in high-demand flows such as ticketing, flash sales, and reservations is a business problem, not a security edge case. Most defences rely on CAPTCHA or aggressive blocking, which hurt real customers more than bots and directly cost conversion and trust.

The approach I have been exploring protects specific high-value actions rather than whole sites, defines what reasonable human behaviour looks like for each one, lets genuine users through with minimal friction, and raises the cost only for automated abuse, with no invasive fingerprinting. This became my Human Protocol project.

Dec 2025

How a single unwrap() took down half the internet

A Cloudflare config update introduced duplicate entries in a feature file that feeds an ML model, pushing it past a hardcoded limit of 200 features. The Rust parser returned an error, and an .unwrap() on that path panicked and crashed the worker threads. Enough panicked threads and the system began to collapse.

This was not Rust's fault; it did exactly what it promised, fail loudly rather than silently. The real culprit was an assumption that the limit "would never be hit." Hardcoded limits are time bombs when unvalidated, .unwrap() has no place in critical production paths, and distributed systems must assume failure, not perfection.
